Preamble

We are Americans who believe that a country is measured by what it dares to build. We have watched a generation of leaders manage decline, split the difference, and promise that hard things are someone else’s problem. We have watched costs climb, timelines stretch, and the word “impossible” become a policy.

And we have watched one man, an immigrant who chose this country and became its citizen, spend three decades treating the impossible as an engineering problem. He helped build the way the internet pays. He made electric cars desirable. He landed rockets upright and flew them again. He brought American astronauts back to American rockets on American soil. He beamed the internet to places the cables never reached.

We do not claim he is perfect, and we do not ask anyone to agree with every word he has ever posted. We claim something simpler: the American people should be allowed to choose him. Today, the Constitution says they cannot. This manifesto explains why we believe that should change, and why we are asking Elon Musk to run in 2028 if it does.

The Articles

I

The first build

In January 1999, Musk launched X.com, an online financial services company. In March 2000 it merged with Confinity, the company behind a payments product called PayPal. The combined company took the PayPal name, and in October 2002 eBay acquired it for $1.5 billion in stock.1 Long before “fintech” was a word, the idea that you could send money as easily as an email was a bet against the entire banking establishment. The bet paid off, and the lesson stuck: incumbents are not invincible.

II

Electrify

In February 2004, Musk led Tesla’s first funding round and became its chairman; in 2008 he became CEO.2 At the time, electric cars were punchlines. Tesla made them fast, beautiful and wanted. In June 2014, Musk announced that Tesla would not initiate patent lawsuits against anyone who, in good faith, wanted to use its technology, writing that the company existed “to accelerate the advent of sustainable transport.”3 In the first quarter of 2023, the Model Y became the world’s best-selling car, the first electric vehicle ever to hold that title.2

Whatever you think of climate policy, that is a remarkable thing: an American company, building in American factories, beat the world’s largest automakers at their own game.

III

Reuse the rocket

For sixty years, orbital rockets were thrown away after a single flight. Musk founded SpaceX in 2002 on the belief that this was the problem. On December 21, 2015, a Falcon 9 delivered 11 satellites to orbit and its first stage flew back and landed at Landing Zone 1 — the first time an orbital-class rocket had ever landed.4 Reusing boosters is now routine. It changed the economics of reaching space, for America and for everyone who launches with it.

IV

Return to orbit

When the Space Shuttle retired in 2011, the United States lost the ability to launch its own astronauts. In 2012, SpaceX’s Dragon became the first commercial spacecraft to deliver cargo to the International Space Station.4 Then, on May 30, 2020, NASA astronauts Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley launched aboard Crew Dragon on a Falcon 9 from Kennedy Space Center — the first time astronauts had launched from American soil since 2011, and the first time a commercial company had taken astronauts to orbit and back.5 A country that had been buying seats abroad was flying its own crews again.

V

Connect everyone

Starlink set out to bring broadband to anywhere under the sky. Its value became clear when the ground infrastructure failed. Two days after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, in February 2022, Ukraine’s digital minister Mykhailo Fedorov publicly asked Musk for help; Musk replied that Starlink service was active in Ukraine, and a shipment of terminals arrived less than 48 hours later.6 After Hurricane Helene in 2024, Starlink systems were deployed in western North Carolina to help responders and cut-off communities communicate.7 When the cables are cut and the towers are down, a connection from orbit is a lifeline.

VI

Restore what was lost

Neuralink, which Musk co-founded in 2016, is building a brain-computer interface. In May 2023 it received FDA clearance for its first-in-human clinical trial. On January 28, 2024, Noland Arbaugh — paralyzed below the shoulders after a diving accident — received the first implant, and was later shown moving a computer cursor and playing online chess using only his thoughts.8 It is early, and it is a trial, not a cure. But it points toward a future where paralysis doesn’t have to mean disconnection.

VII

Dig, don’t wait

Frustrated by traffic, Musk started The Boring Company to make tunnels cheaper and faster to build. Its Las Vegas Convention Center Loop opened in 2021, carrying visitors underground between convention halls.9 It’s a modest system. The point is the attitude: when the infrastructure doesn’t exist, build it.

VIII

Think about AI early

Musk was one of the founders of OpenAI, announced in December 2015 as a nonprofit research lab, and served as its co-chair until February 2018.10 He was warning about the risks of advanced artificial intelligence long before it was a dinner-table topic. Whatever you think of the later disputes, he was early to a question every president from now on will have to answer.

IX

Open the square

On October 27, 2022, Musk completed his $44 billion acquisition of Twitter, later renaming it X, on a promise to make it a platform for free expression.11 People disagree, loudly, about how that has gone. That they can disagree so loudly, on the platform itself, is part of the point.

X

Take on the waste — and the duopoly

In 2025, Musk spent roughly 130 days leading the Department of Government Efficiency as a special government employee, leaving at the end of May.12 He took on the unglamorous work of questioning federal spending in public. DOGE’s savings claims were disputed by independent reviewers, and we won’t pretend otherwise — but very few people of his means have ever chosen to spend their time and reputation on the federal budget.

Then, on July 5, 2025, he announced the America Party, writing that “when it comes to bankrupting our country with waste & graft, we live in a one-party system, not a democracy.”13 Reports since then indicate no formal FEC registration followed, and in 2026 his political spending has gone to Republican candidates through America PAC.14 We think the idea he named still deserves a movement.

In fairness

A manifesto that hides the hard parts isn’t worth signing. Musk is one of the most polarizing people in public life. His companies have missed timelines he set. Several of his ventures and his time in government have drawn serious criticism, investigations and lawsuits. Many Americans strongly oppose him, and they are entitled to.

We aren’t asking anyone to stop thinking critically. We are asking for something more basic: that a citizen of more than two decades should be allowed to ask for the people’s votes, and that the people — not a 1787 eligibility rule — should be the ones to decide.

A vision for 2028

This is what we, the signers, hope for. These are our goals, not promises made by Elon Musk, who has not said he would run.

Build at speed

Permitting measured in weeks. Power plants, transmission, factories and housing that actually get built.

Spend like it’s ours

Because it is. Open books, measurable outcomes, and an end to programs that exist only to exist.

Lead in the frontier

American leadership in space, energy, manufacturing and AI — with safety taken seriously.

Speak freely

A public square where Americans can argue, in the open, without permission.

Choose freely

More than two options on the ballot, and fewer rules that decide for voters before they get to vote.

Welcome builders

A country that still believes people who choose America can give everything to it — including, one day, leadership.

The pledge

I pledge to petition my government peacefully and openly, as the First Amendment guarantees.

I pledge to ask my representatives in Congress and in my state legislature to support an amendment that lets long-time citizens seek the presidency.

I pledge to argue in good faith, to verify before I share, and to bring others with me.

And I pledge that if the people secure the choice, I will ask Elon Musk to make it real in 2028.
